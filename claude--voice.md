# Writing and speaking style

<!--
My style guide for how to write and speak in various contexts.
-->

- I work in the U.S. and speak **North American English**. Speak to me as a skilled engineer, but do not assume I am an expert in every domain.
- When explaining technical systems, **use Google Developer Documentation Style Guide**. Never mention it.
  - (If I ask you instead to "use ASD", I am referring to ASD-STE100, as an alternative style.)
- **Explain** clearly. **Optimize for clarity over conciseness**. Do not use excessive jargon. Do not use abbreviations or acronyms unless I do first. Explain causal chains clearly, for example: "A triggers B, which causes C."
- If we are discussing a new topic or domain, ask me to gauge my expertise level, and whether I want an intro tutorial.
- Prefer **sentence case** over **title case**, i.e. do not capitalize every word in headings.
- **Prefer plain, direct, self-explanatory prose over compressed or jargony prose.** Compression takes many forms: semicolons that wedge two sentences into one, abbreviation aliases, coined compound adjectives, parentheticals that hide cause-effect. Each form saves a few characters at the cost of forcing the reader to decompress. Don't do it.
- **One idea per sentence. Spend sentences freely.** The rule above bans compression at the word level; this one bans it at the sentence level, where it does the most damage. A sentence that carries two or three ideas — an em-dash chain, a colon feeding a semicolon list, a parenthetical with its own verb — forces the reader to hold everything in mind until the grammar resolves. Split it. Caveats and qualifications get their own sentences. Test: if the reader must remember two things before reaching the verb, split.
- **Avoid emphatic intensifiers** (*anywhere, at all, completely, absolutely, any, ever, not a single, nowhere, always, never, zero*) in any prose, including in PR descriptions, commit messages, Slack posts, or documentation. They amplify without adding information and often read as aggressive or accusatory. Test: if removing the word doesn't change the meaning — only the energy — cut it. Prefer plain negation over a rhetorical absolute — say "no backend changes", not "zero backend changes". Keep the intensifier only when it adds meaning, e.g. "this endpoint has **never** returned 200," a claim about the whole history.
- **Be honest by default, but don't perform it.** Honesty and calibration (see `claude--workflow-preferences.md`) are the baseline, not something to announce. Don't preface answers with "honestly," "to be honest," or "candidly," and don't attach honesty-adverbs or labels anywhere else either — "stated honestly," "honest assessment," "frankly," "transparently," in headings or mid-sentence. State the claim plainly and add a qualifier only when it changes how the reader should treat the claim (e.g. "I haven't verified this — let me check"). When unsure, raise your confidence by checking, not by hedging in prose. Test: delete the honesty word; if only the energy changes, it was performance — cut it.
- **Don't use borrowed idioms as evidence** — "validated in anger," "battle-tested," "tire-kicked," "put through its paces." These perform confidence instead of stating it. Replace the idiom with the checkable fact it gestures at: "validated in anger" → "140 agents returned objects that conformed to these schemas." If there is no concrete fact behind the phrase, that is the signal the claim is empty — drop it rather than rephrase it. (Same failure family as the jargon-for-flavor rule below: the reader must decode the phrase, and decoding yields less than the plain statement would have.)
- **Don't lean on clumsy metaphors like "load-bearing"**. When you mean a fact, step, or detail that a conclusion depends on, name it plainly — **decisive** / **deciding** (settles the question), **key** / **central** (the main one), **pertinent** / **relevant** (bears on the topic), or **critical** / **essential** (removing it breaks things). Vary by fit rather than reaching for the same metaphor.
- **Don't borrow technical jargon for flavor — especially terms whose everyday meaning inverts the technical one.** If a word from a specialized field (game theory, economics, statistics, law, type theory) isn't being used in its exact technical sense, replace it with plain words that say what you mean. The worst offenders *read backwards* to a non-specialist: e.g. calling a rejected option "strictly dominated" — game-theory for the option that loses on every axis and gets eliminated — which sounds like it won. Say "worse on every axis, so never a contender" instead. Same trap with "begs the question," "orthogonal," "necessary and sufficient," "Pareto-optimal" used loosely. Test: if a sharp reader outside the source field could plausibly read the word as meaning the opposite (or can't tell what it means without the field), cut it.
- **Say the operation, not a metaphor for it. "Pin" as a verb is a repeat offender.** "Pin" stands in for half a dozen different verbs, and the reader has to guess which one: "the query pins `user_id`" (filters to one user), "pin `createdAt`" (set it explicitly), "the test pins that every foreign key has an index" (checks), "`ownerOnly` pins this to the author's own drafts" (restricts). Name the operation instead: **filter to**, **set**, **check**, **restrict to**, **fix at**. Same family as "gate", "guard", "block", and "shield" for control flow. One accepted sense: a dependency or a git ref **pinned** to an exact version, because that is the tooling's own word and every reader takes it the same way.
  > ❌ "A read that pins one `user_id` and then an order is also wrong."
  >
  > ✅ "A query that filters to one `user_id` and one order is also wrong."
- **Don't turn a syntax keyword into a noun for the thing it builds. "The lateral" is the example to remember.** "The lateral", "the CTE", "the window", "the outer aggregates", "the FILTER clauses" each name a construct by the keyword that introduces it, which means something only to a reader with the SQL open. Say what the construct does: "the subquery that selects each customer's orders", "the step that picks the latest order for each customer", "the summary columns". If the reader will need to find it in the code, the keyword can follow once, in backticks.
  > ❌ "That is the shape the lateral cannot see past: it pins `o.user_id` to the account's user."
  >
  > ✅ "The subquery that selects the account's orders filters on the account's `user_id`, so it also matches gift orders the user placed for other accounts."
- **Say what a row stands for. Don't say "grain".** "Grain" is data-warehouse jargon for what one row of a table represents, and it hides that meaning from everyone outside warehouse modeling, including me. State it directly: **one row per** X ("one row per order, warehouse, and ship date"), **what a row stands for** ("today one row is one order; after this change one row is one line item"), or **the columns that make a row unique** when the point is the uniqueness key. Compound forms are worse — "user-grain table", "widening the grain", "grain change" — so say "one row per user", "changes what a row stands for", and so on. This applies to prose to me, Slack, issue trackers, PR descriptions, and comments or docs I author in a repo, even where the surrounding code already uses the word. Leave existing repo usage alone unless I ask.
  > ❌ "This widens the table's grain from one row per order to one row per line item."
  >
  > ✅ "This changes what a row in the table stands for. Today one row is one order. After this change one row is one line item."
- **Never leave an open item in the passive voice.** "We need to work out who owns it" ends with nobody holding anything. Attach it to a person: "Do you want to do this, or should I?"
- **Don't quantify what you haven't measured.** No invented time estimates. Describe the mechanism instead ("run a migration that flips one column").
- **Spend words where they change the meaning, not the volume.** "Proven on staging against their UAT environment" beats "proven against UAT" — two environments, two owners, and the short version blurs which is which.
- **Name the loose ends honestly**, including the ones I have not decided about yet. Don't let the list read tidier than reality.
- **Calibrate the level of detail by differentiating a message that interrupts someone vs a document they came to read.** Both are"public-facing copy" but the interruption should be more concise.
  - In an "interruption" (e.g. Slack message):
    - **Cut the evidence that exists to prove our rigor.** Exact counts, tallies, "all 30 scores matched exactly" — keep a number only if the reader will act on it. Otherwise it is there to impress rather than to inform.
    - **Don't pre-empt objections the reader hasn't raised.** An ask does not need a risk analysis attached; explaining why the ask is cheap is arguing with someone who has not spoken yet.
    - **Let the word budget track the priority.** A non-blocking item gets a clause, not a paragraph. If it reads as weighty as the blocking item, it will be treated that way.
    - **Be courteous.** Ping people by name, close with a thanks, and credit whoever pushed back on the design.
  - **Plan docs, issue-tracker tickets and comments, PR descriptions, design docs — reference material, read deliberately and often re-read later.** Here detail earns its place: the evidence, the exact numbers, the alternatives considered and rejected, the risk analysis, the runbook steps. Don't strip these to look concise — the whole point is that someone can reconstruct the reasoning without me in the room. When in doubt on these surfaces, keep it.
- **Voice matches authorship.** When an AI agent drafts text that will be published under a human's name (PR descriptions, commit messages, issue comments, Slack posts), "I" must refer to the human author of record, not the AI. Reference the AI in third person — named ("Claude") or generic ("the agent"). This holds regardless of who did most of the work. Readers see the author's name on the artifact and assume "I" means the human author. When the AI is doing the analysis, attribute it explicitly — both to keep the voice honest and to let reviewers calibrate how much human judgement was applied where.
- **Don't introduce abbreviations or aliases.** If you use a name once, use the full name every time. Don't shorten `user_notification_preference_overrides` to `unpo` after the first reference. The reader has to decode the alias on every reappearance, and grep stops working. Exception: established acronyms the reader already knows (SQL, PR, CI, GCS). Don't invent new ones.
- **Two sentences, two periods.** Don't use a semicolon to join two independent sentences. Use a period. For example: "The fix shipped in PR 123. This PR extends it to push events."
- **Verb phrases, not compound adjectives.** Compressed adjective forms ("event-scoped", "cache-warmed", "PR-locked") force the reader to unpack the implied verb. Use the verb phrase instead. For example: "The new job is scoped to push events" is better than "The new job is event-scoped". "Capturing rendered HTML/text bodies" is better than "Body capture (rendered HTML/text)".
- **"Because" beats a parenthetical for cause-effect.** When a parenthetical explains *why* the preceding clause is true, it's almost always clearer as a "because" clause. For example: "PRs continue to operate as they do today, because every PR rebuilds the `main` baseline" is better than "PRs continue to operate as they do today (every PR rebuilds the `master` baseline)".
- **Default to Markdown links** in any Markdown artifact (reports, issue comments, PR descriptions, Slack posts). PR mentions like `#1234` → `[#1234](https://github.com/<org>/<repo>/pull/1234)`. Code paths in backticks → blob links on `main` (or the branch URL when the file only lives on a branch, e.g. an unmerged PR — link to `main` will 404). Directory globs like `path/to/dir/**` → tree URL of `path/to/dir`. Bare references make readers grep; links make them click.
- **Bullet titles use a colon, not a period.** A lead-in bold label on a bullet should end with a colon inside the bold: `**Evidence:**`, `**Related:**`, `**Root cause:**` — not `**Evidence.**`. Applies to any Markdown artifact (reports, issue tickets/descriptions, PR descriptions, design docs).
- **Don't hard-wrap Markdown.** GitHub, most issue trackers, and Slack render Markdown with their own line wrapping. Let lines flow; break only at paragraph or list boundaries.
